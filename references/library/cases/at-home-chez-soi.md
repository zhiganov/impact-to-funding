# C004 — At Home/Chez Soi Housing First demonstration

**Record revision 1; sources inspected 2026-11-08.** Domains: housing, mental
health, supported participation. Historical Canadian demonstration, not a claim
about current housing supply or a universal financial return.

## Context and theory provenance

The Mental Health Commission of Canada (MHCC) implemented a Health Canada-funded
demonstration with local providers in five cities. The national analysis covered
2,148 recruited adults experiencing homelessness/precarious housing and a qualifying
mental disorder, followed for two years during 2009–2013 data collection. This
was not a representative sample of everyone with housing needs.

**Published intervention model; reconstructed short ToC:** immediate subsidised
housing, choice, tenancy rights and separation from compulsory treatment, with
ongoing support matched to need → earlier access and retention → possible further
recovery, functioning and quality-of-life benefits. S1 describes the model; the
short chain here is our reconstruction, not a reproduced original logic-model
diagram. Voluntary treatment does not mean there were no rent/contact conditions.
Do not turn the tested housing-and-support bundle into "housing alone cures illness."

## Findings and limits

- **S2, printed p.17:** Housing First participants spent an average 73% of time
  stably housed versus 32% under treatment as usual over two years. This is share
  of time, not share of participants permanently cured of homelessness. The
  comparator included available services, not an absence of care.
- **S2, pp.27–29:** quality of life and functioning improved more under Housing
  First, but added gains were smaller than housing gains. Mental-health symptoms
  and substance-use problems improved similarly in both groups; Housing First
  did not appear to accelerate those changes over this period. This is not proof
  that housing has no health value or nobody experienced clinical benefit.
- Some participants needed different/more structured support; qualitative accounts
  included isolation. Average cost offsets did not cover intervention costs;
  avoided service use is not automatically a cash saving in government budgets.
  Do not claim the model always pays for itself.

Randomisation supports attribution to the combined offer/support package in the
sample, not separate causal effects of tenancy choice, subsidies or support models.
Interviews were unblinded, much information was self-reported and final follow-up
was unequal. No independent reanalysis or later-evidence review was performed.

S1 includes MHCC staff on the research team; S2 is by the national research team
and published by MHCC. Health Canada reported no design/analysis/publication role,
but MHCC employees participated in research activities, with university investigators
holding ultimate authority. Disclosures and journal peer review do not establish
full evaluator/implementer independence for the whole demonstration.

## Pattern connections and local questions

**ItF interpretation:** C004 revision 1 informs [P002 revision 1](../patterns/keep-outcomes-distinct.md)
because a strong housing-stability result coexists with different clinical and
financial findings. It is not a causal test of the generalised pattern.

A distinct case-level hypothesis is that removing a readiness gate while providing
a concrete resource and continuing support can change access. Do not infer from
this package that removing every condition, or providing housing without support,
will have the same results. Housing stock, affordable tenancies, rights, actual
staff capacity, cultural fit and participant choice matter for adaptation.

Optional [L001/L002 questions, revision 1](../lenses.md): whose housing/security
needs are met, who retains choice and bears costs, and which material/environmental
effects of housing provision remain unexamined? Social findings alone do not
establish ecological performance or complete Doughnut conformance.
