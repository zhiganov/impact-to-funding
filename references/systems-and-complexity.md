# Learning with systems and complexity

**Optional ItF guidance, revision 1, prepared 2026-11-09.** Help people understand
and develop contributions in changing conditions. These are original ItF prompts
informed by the sources below, not an official method, a full systems assessment,
a Cynefin workflow or a test of model reliability.

Use only what helps the current conversation. No named framework, system map,
theory of change, experiment or maturity ladder is required. Exploring interests,
supporting existing work or recognising a relationship can be useful without any
of these. Read the relevant sections through the host's tools; do not make the
person open this Markdown file as a substitute for using the skill.

## Notice when a wider view helps

Consider interactions when, for example, several actors influence the result,
conditions are changing, a response creates new pressures, or an effect arrives
later or elsewhere. Ask only the few questions that matter. Many actors, a large
problem or missing information alone do not establish how a system behaves.
An ordinary information gap may need a fact or expert judgement, not a complexity
label. Different activities within one project may need different approaches.

Keep a working boundary: whose experience, which relationships, what place and
time period are relevant, and what might be excluded? Consider history, power,
incentives, capacity and who can change what. A boundary is a practical choice,
not proof that outside influences disappear. Do not invent actors' views, agreed
roles or a consensus about success.

## Keep causal hypotheses revisable

Where useful, describe how a contribution could help and why that explanation
might be wrong. A pathway can branch, interact with other pathways or change over
time. Distinguish an observed relationship, a proposed mechanism and a hoped-for
outcome. Keep alternatives and material assumptions visible, including what might
happen without the contribution. A coherent account is not causal proof.

Retain the person's direction or a shared project's aspirations without pretending
that all steps or a final state are predictable. Look for evidence that challenges
the explanation, not just signs that can be fitted into it. Revise the theory with
its version and reason for change; do not rewrite an earlier expectation as though
it had predicted the result. Apply the usual [claim rules](claims.md).

## Distinguish feedback dynamics from receiving opinions

A feedback loop exists when an effect changes conditions that influence the
process again. Hearing someone's feedback is valuable, but is not by itself
evidence of such a loop. Ask how the response gets back into practice and what
authority, relationships or capacity make that possible.

Where relevant, examine whether an effect reinforces a change or counteracts it.
Reinforcing does not mean beneficial, and balancing does not mean harmful. Consider
delays, limits and effects outside the immediate activity: an early improvement
may create a later burden, and more effort need not produce proportionally more
benefit. Label a suggested loop as a hypothesis unless observations support it;
do not invent a delay, threshold, direction or magnitude.

## Make room for emergence

Interactions can produce patterns, roles or outcomes that were not specified by
individual participants or apparent from their separate activities. Not every
surprise establishes emergence; describe what was actually noticed and what is
still unexplained. Unexpected effects can be helpful, harmful or contested.

Attend to changes in relationships, agency, exclusion and participation as well as
planned indicators. Do not force an unexpected outcome into the original pathway,
claim exclusive credit for it or dismiss it because it was not a target. Emergence
does not mean that causes cannot be investigated, that evidence is unnecessary or
that all perspectives agree. Actual affected people's judgements remain distinct
from the agent's interpretation.

## Connect learning to a possible response

When further learning is useful, connect a proportionate question to a response
someone could actually make. Reuse permitted records and existing review points
before proposing collection. A few sentences may cover:

- What is the current hypothesis or uncertainty, and why does learning matter now?
- What observations, including harms or contrary evidence, would support revising
  it? Who might see something different, and whose perspective remains missing?
- Who could interpret and act on that learning, with what authority, capacity,
  consent and access? Unknown responsibilities stay unknown, not assigned by AI.
- When would it be useful to review, and what would justify continuing, changing,
  supporting existing work, deferring or stopping?

An authorised small action can sometimes help learning, but a conversation,
existing observation or an unresolved question may be enough. Do not launch an
experiment, monitoring or outreach merely because it could yield information.
If action is proposed, identify a bounded scope, burden, possible harms and stop
conditions; small or reversible does not automatically mean safe. Do not make
essential help conditional on participation. Changing a question, method or plan
needs an explicit reason, not an endless cycle of new prerequisites.

## Keep Cynefin distinct and optional

The Cynefin Company's overview presents the framework as support for making sense
of context and choosing appropriate action, not treating every challenge alike.
Snowden's 2017 article distinguishes causal and dispositional approaches and
argues for their different boundaries. That is an attributed author position,
not a consensus definition of all systems thinking or a universal ranking.

These ItF notes do not equate systems thinking with deterministic prediction, or
treat complexity as proof that causes do not exist. They do not assign Cynefin
domains from a short description or silently combine its method with another.
A named-method request may warrant consulting its relevant authoritative guidance
within the actual task scope; these brief notes do not establish method competence,
conformance or a complete Cynefin workflow. People may decline the perspective.

## Entirely fictional illustration

A volunteer group considers an easier issue-reporting form. One hypothesis is that
it helps people get needs addressed. Another is that more reports overwhelm the
available responders; longer waits might then encourage duplicate reports and
increase the backlog. With no observations supplied, this is a proposed loop,
not a finding. Useful help might be clarifying response capacity or supporting
existing triage rather than building more collection. Unexpected peer assistance
would deserve attention if observed, but should not be claimed in advance. No
trial, outcome or participant agreement is established by this authored example.
