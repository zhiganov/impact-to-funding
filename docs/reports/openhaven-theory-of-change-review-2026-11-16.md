# OpenHaven: a reconstructed theory of change

**Discussion draft for the OpenHaven team · 16 November 2026**

This note reconstructs how OpenHaven's published work could lead to change.
It draws on public pages and separates stated intentions, inferred pathways,
available observations and missing evidence.

The analysis was prepared with AI assistance using Impact to Funding's
[optional reconstruction playbook](https://github.com/zhiganov/impact-to-funding/blob/ef3ade70b70f1387eed2b7ed13f77028f9e2e5d0/playbooks/reconstruct-theory-of-change.md).
It is offered for discussion, rather than as an independent evaluation or a
statement endorsed by OpenHaven.

## Main finding

**OpenHaven's clearest pathway is helping people choose technology. Its larger
ambition—reducing ecosystem fragmentation—requires a second pathway involving
coordination and implementation. The Navigator alone cannot supply that.**

The inspected pages displayed `v0.12.0 · aab0295`. That is the site's displayed
version, not an independently verified source-code revision. The pages were
retrieved on 16 November 2026.

## 1. Stated intention

OpenHaven aims to make decentralized technology understandable and actionable,
while supporting technical and social convergence.

Its [stakeholder brief](https://openhaven.net/brief) describes several audiences:
community coordinators choosing tools, developers building applications, and
coalitions seeking a shared evidence base.

The brief presents navigation as part of a wider ambition: a shared reference
point, a collective voice and support for collaboration across fragmented
efforts. The [homepage](https://openhaven.net/) emphasizes documenting the
landscape, making it navigable and advocating for interoperability.

## 2. Inferred pathways

The pathways below are analytical reconstructions, rather than demonstrated
outcomes.

| Pathway | How change could happen | Critical dependency |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Better technology choices | Research → needs-based navigation → relevant options → suitable adoption → better coordination | Someone must assess local fit, deploy the tool, support users and maintain it. |
| Less ecosystem fragmentation | Shared map → teams identify complementary work → agree joint priorities → implement interoperability | Teams need compatible incentives, authority, time and funding. |
| More trustworthy shared knowledge | Published entries → use and corrections → improved research → more useful guidance | Contributors must supply meaningful corrections, and reviewers need capacity to resolve them. |

The [Navigator](https://openhaven.net/navigator),
[research pages](https://openhaven.net/research) and
[contribution form](https://openhaven.net/contribute) provide visible starting
points. The later transitions remain hypotheses within the inspected material.

The [process page](https://openhaven.net/process) describes research, review
and update triggers. It supports an explanation of how the knowledge base is
intended to improve. The inspected pages do not establish how often that feedback
loop operates or what decisions it has changed.

OpenHaven's contribution is distinct from the work of tool maintainers,
implementers, community coordinators and participants. These actors would share
responsibility for adoption and any later coordination outcomes.

## 3. Three useful findings

### A. Discovery can succeed while convergence fails

People might each find a suitable tool while choosing different, incompatible
systems. That would be a discovery success without reducing fragmentation.

OpenHaven's own ["Why" page](https://openhaven.net/why) recognizes that visibility
needs someone who acts on it. It also notes that better visibility can reveal
competition and disagreement without resolving them.

The reconstruction makes the consequence explicit:

> Tool-selection outcomes and interoperability outcomes need separate
> explanations.

A shared map could help teams find opportunities to work together. Turning an
opportunity into interoperability requires further work: agreeing a problem,
allocating capacity, making technical choices and sustaining integration.

This distinction allows both pathways to be valuable without counting one as
evidence of the other.

### B. “Verified” describes different levels of assurance across the pages

The [brief](https://openhaven.net/brief), dated 27 February 2026, promises
expert-verified, human-reviewed data. The
[process page](https://openhaven.net/process) describes internal Research Lead
review as today's baseline. The [beta page](https://openhaven.net/beta) says data
is “reviewed, but not yet deeply verified,” with additional confirmation
mechanisms being added.

These statements describe different levels of assurance. They do not establish
that the data is inaccurate, or explain whether the wording reflects a change
in plans. No historical versions were compared.

A useful distinction is:

> Reviewed information can support exploration; suitability for a particular
> deployment needs its own assessment.

That distinction matters especially for the brief's high-stakes journalism
example. Governance information is useful, but the
[capture-risk methodology](https://openhaven.net/definitions/capture-risk)
measures concentration of control—not operational security for a particular
journalist.

A governance assessment, a capability claim and a deployment recommendation
therefore answer different questions.

### C. Selection starts within a chosen architectural direction

OpenHaven excludes fully captured platforms from its primary discovery surfaces,
as explained in its
[capture-risk methodology](https://openhaven.net/definitions/capture-risk) and
[baseline comparison](https://openhaven.net/definitions/baseline-comparison).

Its theory therefore fits:

> Help people find suitable open/decentralized options.

That is narrower than:

> Determine the best technology for any coordination problem.

Whether changing architecture is worth migration, training and maintenance costs
remains a separate question. For example, a community could value openness while
also needing a tool its members can access and support today. This is a possible
trade-off, not an observed OpenHaven user experience.

The narrower framing makes the project's scope explicit while preserving the
value of its architectural direction.

## 4. Trace one outcome: less ecosystem fragmentation

| Connection | Proposed mechanism | What the inspected material supports |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Research → a shared view of capabilities | Comparable descriptions make overlaps, differences and possible connections easier to see. | Published navigation, research and a detailed tool entry are visible. The whole dataset was not audited. |
| Shared view → opportunities for collaboration | Teams can identify complementary capabilities or duplicated effort. | The brief explains this intended use. No attributable example of such a decision was found in the inspected pages. |
| Opportunity → agreed joint work | People with authority and capacity decide to integrate or coordinate. | This is an inferred dependency. A directory cannot supply agreement, resources or aligned incentives. |
| Joint work → functioning interoperability | Teams implement, test and maintain compatible systems. | No implementation outcome attributable to OpenHaven was established by this review. |
| Interoperability → better coordination | People can work across systems with less friction or dependence on a single provider. | This remains an intended benefit. Adoption, accessibility, support and governance would also affect the result. |

The strongest missing connection is between identifying an opportunity and
undertaking sustained joint work.

Without OpenHaven, some actors might discover tools through existing directories,
documentation or relationships. Its additional contribution could be a more
usable comparison, a shared reference or lower research effort. The inspected
material does not establish the size of that difference.

## 5. Attributed experience and observed material

The audience personas in the brief are design descriptions, not user testimony.
No attributable adoption or coordination outcomes were found in the inspected
pages. That finding is limited to this source set.

The review directly observed:

- published statements of purpose and intended audiences;
- needs-based navigation content;
- research and process guidance;
- a contribution form;
- a detailed [DIDs entry](https://openhaven.net/protocols/dids), including a
  last-investigated date, capability descriptions and relationship information;
- published explanations of capture-risk scoring and discovery scope.

These observations establish available material. They do not independently
confirm its underlying claims or its effects on users.

## 6. A tighter reconstructed theory

> OpenHaven organizes research around human needs so communities and builders can
> identify suitable decentralized technologies. Adoption depends on local
> implementation and support. Ecosystem convergence additionally depends on
> teams using that shared knowledge to undertake coordinated work.

This account preserves the wider ambition while distinguishing three outcomes:

- informed technology selection;
- successful adoption;
- ecosystem convergence.

Each can matter in its own right. Progress on one should retain its own meaning
rather than stand in for the others.

The central open question is:

> Who turns a useful recommendation into an adopted, supported solution—and who
> turns a visible connection between projects into sustained interoperability work?

That question concerns the pathway from knowledge to action. It does not assume
that OpenHaven itself must deliver every part of it.

## 7. What this exercise says about the reconstruction playbook

This application produced more than a mission summary. It separated outcomes
that could otherwise blur together and identified work that depends on actors
beyond the Navigator.

That changes what “working” would mean for each pathway. It provides a useful
discussion result, rather than evidence that the playbook reliably helps teams
or that OpenHaven has achieved the outcomes described.

This was a manual application in the same conversation as the playbook's
development. It did not test fresh-session skill routing or an installed copy.

## Limits

- This is an AI-assisted reconstruction from public pages, not an independent
  impact evaluation.
- The full Matrix exceeded the retrieval tool's response-size limit. That is a
  limit of this review, rather than a finding about the website.
- Interactive Navigator workflows, data exports and underlying source references
  were not independently tested or audited.
- Publication, retrieval, import and last-investigated dates describe different
  things. A page retrieved today is not necessarily newly researched.
- No team members or users were interviewed. The analysis supplies no affected
  person's agreement and makes no inference about private intentions.
- Possible dependencies and trade-offs are analytical hypotheses, not evidence
  that harm or failure occurred.
