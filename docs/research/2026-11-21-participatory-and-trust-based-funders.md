# How participatory and trust-based funders decide, and how to reach one

Research from 2026-11-21. It asks how funders that hand decisions to others, or that give with little paperwork, choose who gets money. It also asks how a small builder gets in front of one.

**Sources.** Most findings come from [Hey Nabu](../hey-nabu-assessment.md), asked on 2026-11-21 (server version 0.17.0), mostly its Systemic Philanthropy expert. Each Hey Nabu entry is its own summary of a source, read on the date it gives. The open-source examples come from a web search on the same day. None of the linked pages was opened for this note. Check each funder's own page before relying on terms, amounts or windows.

## Short answer

Two different things go by these names:

- **Participatory funds** let people other than the funder decide: past grantees, community panels or the applicants themselves.
- **Trust-based funders** keep the decision but give unrestricted, multi-year money with light paperwork. Many of them don't take applications at all.

Both mostly find new grantees through people they already fund. A builder gets in front of them by being known to those people, and by watching for the few open windows.

## How participatory funds decide

The Climate Justice Resilience Fund ran six pilots in 2024 and 2025, moving $4.3 million, and [published its methods](https://www.cjrfund.org/lessons-from-our-pgm-pilots/):

- **Applicants vote.** In its 2024 global open call, 513 groups expressed interest and 274 applied. 175 shortlisted applicants each read 8 to 12 proposals over two rounds and voted. They chose four organisations for multi-year $200,000 grants. Staff shaped the voting groups to guarantee one African winner.
- **Partners nominate.** In the Bay of Bengal round, seven current and former partners each nominated three organisations, for a $600 honorarium. Staff scored the applications, and the board approved all eight staff recommendations.
- **Winners pass it on.** In 2025, each of the four open-call winners picked two groups in its own network for $15,000.
- **Its own cost note:** each mapping workshop cost about $35,000, of which facilitators took about $17,000. Participants were paid $300 to $350.

Other funds that devolve decisions:

- **[Mama Cash Resilience Fund](https://www.mamacash.org/funds/resilience-fund/):**
  - **Who decides:** a committee of 11 activists decides all new grants.
  - **Grants:** €5,000 to €50,000 a year, often renewed for up to ten years. In 2024 it made 166 grants after nearly 2,500 applications.
  - **Who can apply:** groups led by the women, girls, trans or intersex people they work with. Small, informal and unregistered groups qualify.
  - **When:** it takes no new applicants in 2026. Letters of interest usually open in February.
- **[North Star Fund](https://northstarfund.org/2026/10/new-cfc-members/):** committees of about forty community organisers, many of them current or former grantees, allocated over $3 million to 150+ groups in 2024 and 2025.
- **[Giving Projects](https://socialjusticefund.org/giving-projects/) (Social Justice Fund):** a six-month cohort that raises money together and then decides the grants. People from applicant organisations may join.

**Two cautions from the sources:**

- **Votes can favour the well connected.** Practitioners report peers voting for applicants they know. That's documented in single cases, not measured. The one experiment cited found popular voting less biased against small organisations than rubric scoring.
- **"Participatory" can mean advising, not deciding.** At some pooled funds, donors or staff keep the last word. Hey Nabu marks this claim as disputed. Read who actually decides.

## How trust-based funders decide

The [Trust-Based Philanthropy Project](https://www.trustbasedphilanthropy.org/) names six practices:
- multi-year unrestricted funding;
- doing the homework;
- simpler paperwork;
- being transparent and responsive;
- asking for and acting on feedback;
- support beyond the cheque.

It says 6 in 10 grantmakers have embraced them, citing a 2025 study by Grantmakers for Effective Organizations. Its own 2024 survey found most practitioners don't use the label publicly.

**Examples:**

- **[Tudor Trust](https://www.tudortrust.org.uk/our-work/our-grant-making/) (UK):** no written application. It finds organisations through its current grant partners, desk research and its own maps of each field, then invites them. Conversations replace the application. Grants run from £100,000 to £1 million, usually multi-year and core.
- **[Marguerite Casey Foundation](https://caseygrants.org/) (US):** general operating support by invitation, up to 25% of a budget over five years.
- **The Shears Foundation (UK):** a small family funder whose [case study](https://betterfunding.org/case-study-the-shears-foundation-how-thoughtful-grant-making-supports-both-funders-and-partners/) shows simpler applications and reporting, and unrestricted funding.

**Lists of funders who have committed to lighter, flexible giving:**

- **[IVAR's Open and Trusting Grant-making](https://www.ivar.org.uk/open-and-trusting/) (UK):** each funder publishes how it meets eight commitments, including "we will give unrestricted funding; if we can't, we will make our funding as flexible as possible". Charities report back on funders every two years.
- **[Meet the Moment](https://www.trustbasedphilanthropy.org/meet-the-moment) (US):** a pledge with more than 200 signatories by January 2026, mostly small and midsize funders. No page read checks what signers did.

**The argument against.** A City Journal op-ed, "Just Trust Us" (December 2023), argued that too many groups apply to give them all a role in decisions. Nonprofit Quarterly replied that foundation money is held for the public. Neither names a participatory fund that failed.

## Open-source and software examples (web)

Hey Nabu held one 2022 podcast on Gitcoin and nothing current. The web search found:

- **[Gitcoin Grants](https://grants.gitcoin.co):** community-voted funding through quadratic funding, where many small donations decide how a matching pool is split. It also has retroactive rounds that pay for proven past work. Gitcoin says it has distributed over $60 million to 3,700+ projects. Its GG23 round in April 2025 gave $1.4 million across 265 projects. These figures are Gitcoin's own, and some pages describe older rounds, so check round dates.
- **[FundOSS](https://opencollective.com/fundoss)** on Open Collective, another quadratic-funding effort for open source. Its current status wasn't checked.

## How a small builder gets in front of them

- **Be known to people already funded.** Tudor finds grantees through its partners. CJRF asked partners to nominate. Its winners chose the next grantees. Working with funded peers is the main route in.
- **Get onto the field's maps.** Tudor and CJRF both build maps of who works on what. Being visible in a field's shared directories and convenings puts a builder on them.
- **Watch the open windows.** Some participatory funds open once a year, such as Mama Cash's February window. Applicant-voted calls also mean reviewing other applicants' proposals.
- **Use the signatory lists.** IVAR's and Meet the Moment's lists show which funders have committed to lighter processes.
- **Check eligibility first.** Most of these funders fund organisations or groups, often with a specific community focus. Individual builders may need a fiscal host or an organisation to apply through.

## Gaps

- Hey Nabu held almost nothing on participatory funding for software or open-source builders. The Gitcoin and FundOSS entries come from the web. A coverage request was filed with Hey Nabu.
- No source here measures how often an unknown applicant reaches an invitation-only funder.
- This note covers one question on one day. It isn't a full list of funders.
