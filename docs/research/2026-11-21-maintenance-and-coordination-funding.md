# Who pays for maintenance and coordination

Research from 2026-11-21. It asks which funders pay to keep open tools and community coordination running, rather than to build new things. It's meant for builders writing a support request, and for ItF's funder-research guidance.

**Sources.** Most findings come from [Hey Nabu](../hey-nabu-assessment.md), asked on 2026-11-21 (server version 0.17.0), from its Systemic Philanthropy, System Change, Regenerative AI and Reaching People experts. Each Hey Nabu entry is its own summary of a source, read on the date it gives. The rest come from a web search on the same day. None of the linked pages was opened for this note. Check each funder's own page before relying on terms, amounts or deadlines.

## Short answer

Money for upkeep and coordination is scarce. Funders pay for new work and research far more often than for keeping things running. A few funders pay for upkeep directly. The strongest framing for a request is to name the gap between who uses a tool and who pays for it.

## How thin the money is

From Hey Nabu (Systemic Philanthropy):

- Invest in Open Infrastructure tracked $663 million to 94 open infrastructures from 2001 to 2026, across 47 funders. $192 million (29%) reached the infrastructures directly. $411 million funded work that depends on them. Only $64 million of the direct money paid to keep them running.
- Creative Commons sees $28.70 of dependent research for every dollar it receives directly. DOAJ sees $23.90 and RRID $22.60.
- Nine countries fund research that depends on arXiv. One funds arXiv's own grants.
- Of grant money reaching open infrastructures directly, nearly two-thirds pays for research and development and about 20% for operations. That split covers grants only. Many infrastructures, most North American ones, earn more from services than from grants.
- Where upkeep is secure, it's often not grant-funded. ROR's core staff and running costs are in-kind commitments from California Digital Library, Crossref and DataCite. Time-limited money pays only for time-limited projects.

From Hey Nabu (System Change), on coordination. The checking agents disagreed on this reading, so Hey Nabu marks it disputed:

- Bridgespan surveyed 310 pooled funder collaboratives from 2022 to 2024. Most had their operating costs covered for their first three to five years. Very few had an endowment or a board covering those costs after that.
- Field catalysts in Bridgespan's 2023 study had a median budget of $5 million and a median annual funding gap of $2.5 million. 62% of new efforts had gaps of half their budget or more.
- RE-AMP, a Midwest climate network, shows both halves. Other funders were reluctant to pay for its process in its first year. By 2007, other funders covered 75% of its budget, and 25 foundations contributed in its first ten years.

What was found against this reading:

- **RE-AMP cuts the other way.** Read past its first year, its infrastructure budget grew from $350,000 to $900,000 by 2014, with more than $24 million raised from 2004 to 2013. Bridgespan's collaboratives are pooled funds, not coordinating bodies, so its figure is about running a pooled fund. What's scarce is money to keep coordination going, not money to start it. The field-catalyst funding gap still stands.
- **Coordination can win without funding.** In a California coalition case, Dignity Not Detention had no funding but carried a policy win through 2017 and 2019 on its members' skills and its relationship with a bill's sponsor. Elsewhere, a funded coordinator helped. So funded coordination is one way a coalition keeps going, not the only one.
- **Who says coordination needs funding:** intermediaries that host pooled funds, such as NEO Philanthropy, ask funders to pay for their own staffing. Their president says otherwise the costs pass to the projects they sponsor. They gain from the claim being true, which doesn't make it false.

A pattern the research matches: each funder's separate proposal and reporting format draws on the same few people who hold a network together. They burn out, and the network loses what they knew. This is a version of the tragedy of the commons.

The field's own example: the [Participatory Grantmaking Community](https://www.participatorygrantmaking.org/), about 2,000 people in 73 countries, now runs on an estimated £5,500 a year. One funder, Impatience Ltd, gave £193,000 of its £257,000 total between 2021 and 2024, and nothing since.

Grantmakers.io, a free search over US foundation tax filings, runs on about $200 a year, from voluntary gifts and a donated search service.

## What this means for a request

- **Ask for operations as operations.** Name upkeep, maintenance and coordination as costs in their own right, not as overhead on new work.
- **Name the vulnerability.** The Sovereign Tech Fund asks what part of a project is underfunded and what that gap risks. That question works for any funder.
- **Show who uses it against who pays.** The dependent-use ratios above are the field's own evidence for that argument.
- **Plan past year three.** Coordination funding usually covers the first three to five years. Say how the work is paid for after that.

## Gaps

- The research behind Hey Nabu hasn't covered smaller or EU open-source grant programmes. The NLnet, NumFOCUS and Alpha-Omega entries come from the web. A coverage request was filed for them.
- Three questions haven't been researched yet. One is whether paying for the wellbeing of backbone staff keeps coordination funded for longer.
- This note covers one question on one day. It isn't a full list of funders.
