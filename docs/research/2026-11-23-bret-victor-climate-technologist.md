# What can a technologist do about climate change?

A reading note from 2026-11-23 on Bret Victor's essay [What can a technologist do about climate change? A personal view](https://worrydream.com/ClimateChange/) (November 2015), and what it suggests for ItF and [Funding to Impact (FtI)](https://itfti.org/fti).

**Source.** The essay's web page, read in full on 2026-11-23, apart from its interactive examples and linked sources. Quotes are the essay's own words. It's one person's view from 2015; its figures are 2015 figures.

## What it is

A map of where technologists could help with climate change, written for people in the tech industry thinking about their careers. Victor leaves out policy, and says technologists "create options for policy-makers". The sections:

- **Funding:** public clean-energy funding is "a pittance" next to the scale of the problem, and venture funding dried up after 2011.
- **Producing, moving and consuming energy:** many contributions sit around the core technology, not in it. Examples are cheaper mounting and tracking for solar, software that places wind turbines, grid protocols ("TCP/IP for energy"), storage and demand flexibility.
- **Tools for scientists and engineers:** better languages for technical computing, modelling and control. He argues these matter enormously, but their effect is "not one that is easily visible or measured".
- **Tools for problem-finding and evaluating ideas.**
- **Media for understanding situations:** model-driven debate, reading and authoring.
- **Morale, and where engineers spend their time.**

## Findings that matter here

**Finding the problem is a problem.** Victor describes an inventor who wants to click on a field and see its open problems, who's working on what, the fringe ideas, the process bottlenecks, what dominates cost and what limits adoption. "None of this information is at her fingertips." Organisations publish successes, not roadblocks: "What if there were some way Tesla could reveal their open problems?"

**Evaluating an idea takes weeks.** Before working on an idea, she needs to know whether the improvement is significant, feasible and affordable, who'd use it, and which metrics even matter. He wants quick estimates, like an instant manufacturing quote inside a design tool.

**Claims without calculations.** In the Cash for Clunkers debate, claims "offered no numbers", numbers had no context, and "never were readers shown the calculations behind any numbers." His alternative is a document with a model inside: readers change the assumptions and see the consequences. He asks for "every claim to be accompanied by an explorable analysis, and every statistic to be linked to a primary source."

**Cite models, not only data.** The editors of Al Gore's *Our Choice* rejected interactive graphics because each number needed a prior citation, even when the graphic used the scientists' own formula. We lack standards for citing models, and readers need to learn to read one.

**Valuable work can be hard to measure.** Foundational technology "appears essential only in retrospect" and is "rarely sought out (or funded!)".

**Aim at people with leverage.** Energy feedback for consumers mostly failed. Tools for people who run whole systems, such as waste-collection routing, cut fuel use in half.

**Morale.** Despair makes people turn away from the problem. Keeping people at hard work is a problem in its own right.

**Where engineers go.** Thousands of engineers work on picture-sharing apps while public services go unstaffed. "The world that scrolls past you on Twitter is not the real world."

## What this suggests for ItF and FtI

These are suggestions drawn from the essay, not findings about ItF or FtI:

- **Organisations could publish bottlenecks, not only tasks.** FtI's needs format describes help an organisation wants. Adding what's stuck, what limits adoption and what was tried would let builders with ideas find where they fit. (Filed for FtI.)
- **A quick "is it worth doing" step.** ItF helps people choose a contribution and accepts stopping as a result. A short sizing step, asking whether the effect would be significant, feasible and wanted, could come before someone commits time.
- **Show the calculation.** ItF's claim checks hold claims to evidence and date their sources. The next step is showing how a number was worked out and what it assumes. That's a direction for a visual impact card ([#144](https://github.com/zhiganov/impact-to-funding/issues/144)).
- **Funders could publish how they decide.** A funder file could carry its criteria and how they're weighed, a funder's model for applicants to read.
- **Don't let evidence rules punish infrastructure.** Maintenance and tools often have indirect effects. ItF should help people argue honestly for them without inventing numbers. This fits the [maintenance funding research](2026-11-21-maintenance-and-coordination-funding.md).
- **Peers keep people going.** Guilds and mentors matter for morale, not only for skills.

## Gaps

- The essay is from 2015. Its funding figures and examples are out of date, and several companies it names have since closed or changed.
- It's about climate technology. The parts used here are general, but it says nothing about small builders or grant funding.
- The interactive examples and the 400 linked sources weren't explored.
